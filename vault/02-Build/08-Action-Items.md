# 2.8 — Action items and exercises

**8 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Why this is worth a whole lesson

Because completion rate is the thing that determines whether your course succeeds
commercially, and action items are the main lever on completion rate.

The chain: a customer who *does* the course gets a result. A customer who gets a result
tells people, does not refund, and becomes a genuine testimonial
([[05-Bonus/04-Collecting-Testimonials]]). A customer who reads passively and does nothing
refunds at some rate and recommends you to nobody.

Free courses have famously dismal completion rates. Paid ones do better, and paid ones
with concrete action items do better still — because "read this" has no completion state
and "produce this" does.

---

## What makes an action item work

Four properties. All four, not three.

**1. Specific.** The reader knows exactly what to do without interpreting.

**2. Completable in under 20 minutes.** Longer and it gets deferred; deferred means never.

**3. Produces something.** A file, a photo, a list, a decision written down. Something
that exists afterwards.

**4. Uses what the lesson just taught.** Otherwise it is homework, not practice.

---

## Bad and good

| Bad | Why it fails | Good |
|-----|--------------|------|
| Think about your lighting. | No output, no completion state | Photograph one product by a window and one under a ceiling light. Put them side by side. |
| Consider your pricing strategy. | Vague, unbounded | Write three prices for your course in `My Course.md`: too cheap, right, too expensive. Circle the middle one. |
| Practise these techniques. | Which? For how long? | Edit three photos using only Exposure, White Balance and Highlights. Time yourself. |
| Review the concepts covered. | Not an action | List the two ideas from this lesson you will use this week. |
| Build your email list. | Too big, spans weeks | Write your opt-in sentence. One line, in `My Course.md`. |

Notice that every "good" version names a file or a physical output. That is not
coincidence — it is the mechanism.

---

## Three to five per lesson

Fewer than three and the lesson feels passive. More than five and the reader picks none,
because a long list reads as a project.

Order them so the first one is the smallest. A reader who does item one usually does item
two; a reader who bounces off item one does none.

---

## Exercises, for the bigger ones

Some things need more than 20 minutes. Make those **exercises** rather than action items,
and give them their own file:

```
04-Editing/
  01-The-Three-Adjustments.md
  02-Getting-Consistency.md
  Exercise-Reshoot-Your-Range.md
```

An exercise file should contain:

- **What you will produce** — stated first
- **How long it takes** — honestly
- **What you need** — before they start, not discovered halfway
- **The steps** — numbered
- **What good looks like** — so they can self-assess
- **Common problems** — and fixes

That last section is what stops an exercise becoming a support ticket.

---

## Checklists

For any multi-step process, give a checklist as a separate file in `Templates/`.

Checklists are among the most-valued things in a course and among the fastest to produce,
because you are converting something you already wrote into a different shape. Many
customers will tell you the checklist was the most useful part.

```markdown
# Pre-shoot checklist

- [ ] Shooting between 10am and 3pm
- [ ] Within 1m of a window
- [ ] Ceiling lights OFF
- [ ] White card positioned opposite the window
- [ ] Phone HDR off
- [ ] Lens wiped
```

Use `- [ ]` — Obsidian renders these as tickable boxes, which is satisfying in a way that
measurably increases the chance people work through them.

---

## The "prove it worked" item

Worth adding at the end of each module: a way for the reader to check they actually got
the result.

> **Before moving on:** you should have 10 photos of the same product range that look
> like they were taken at the same time. If they do not, the problem is almost always
> that the ceiling lights were on for some of them — reshoot those.

This does two useful things. It catches people who have moved on without succeeding, and
it gives them a diagnosis rather than a vague sense of failure.

---

## The completion arc

Design the action items so that after the final module, the reader has *built the thing*.

Not "understands how to build the thing" — has built it. If your course is about product
photography, the final action item should leave them with a finished, reshot listing.

Work backwards from that. If the action items across your course do not add up to a
finished output, something is missing.

---

## Action Items

1. **Audit every lesson.** Mark any without action items, and any whose action items fail
   the four-property test.
2. **Rewrite the failures.** Every one must name a specific output.
3. **Identify your exercises** — anything over 20 minutes — and give each its own file
   with the six-part structure.
4. **Write one checklist** for your most multi-step process. Put it in `Templates/`.
5. **Trace the completion arc.** List what a reader has produced after every module. If
   the end is not a finished thing, fix the gaps.
