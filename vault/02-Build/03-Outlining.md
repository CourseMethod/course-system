# 2.3 — Outlining: modules and lessons

**14 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Why outlining first is not optional

Because writing without an outline produces material you delete.

You will write three lessons, realise lesson two should come after lesson three, discover
that both assume something you have not explained, and rewrite all of it. That is a lost
week, and it is entirely avoidable by spending ninety minutes on structure first.

An outline is also what makes the writing *tractable*. "Write a course" is paralysing.
"Write 800 words on why the obvious approach fails" is a Tuesday evening.

---

## Start from the outcome, work backwards

You have one outcome from [[01-Mindset/08-Committing-To-Scope]]:

> By the end, they will be able to **[X]**.

Now ask, repeatedly: **what must be true immediately before that?**

Worked example — *photograph your Etsy products so they look professional*:

- To do that, they must be able to **edit a photo to look consistent**
- To do that, they must have **a photo that is technically decent** to start with
- To do that, they must **control the light**
- To do that, they must **understand why their current photos look wrong**
- To do that, they must **know what "professional" actually means** in this context

Reverse it, and you have your module order:

1. What good looks like (and why yours does not)
2. Controlling light
3. Taking the shot
4. Editing for consistency
5. Putting it to work on the listing

This is far more reliable than listing everything you know and grouping it. Working
backwards from the outcome guarantees every lesson is load-bearing.

---

## Modules: 3 to 6, each one phase

A module is a phase of the journey, not a category of knowledge.

| Category-based (weak) | Phase-based (strong) |
|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Equipment | Working out what is wrong |
| Theory | Fixing the light |
| Software | Taking better shots |
| Business | Making them consistent |

The left column organises around *your* mental model. The right organises around what the
reader does next, which is what they need.

Three to six modules. Fewer than three usually means insufficient structure; more than six
in a first course usually means scope creep came back.

---

## Lessons: one idea each

Each lesson does **one** thing. The test: can you write its title as a specific claim or
instruction?

| Weak title | Strong title |
|------------|--------------|
| Lighting | Why window light beats every lamp you own |
| Introduction to editing | The three adjustments that fix 90% of photos |
| Tips and tricks | The five mistakes that make a photo look amateur |
| Advanced techniques | Shooting a whole product range in one session |

Strong titles make the lesson easy to write, because the title already tells you what
belongs in it and what does not.

**3 to 7 lessons per module.** If a module has ten, it is probably two modules. If it has
one, it is probably a section of another lesson.

---

## The outlining session

Ninety minutes. One sitting if you can.

### Step 1 — Brain dump (20 min)

Every possible lesson, one per line, no filtering, no order. Aim for 40. You will cut
most of them.

### Step 2 — Backwards chain (15 min)

Do the "what must be true before that?" exercise above. Write the phases.

### Step 3 — Sort (20 min)

Put each dumped item under a phase. Three things will happen, all useful:

- Some items fit nowhere → they are out of scope. Move them to `Course Two.md`.
- Some phases have nothing → either the phase is unnecessary, or you have a gap you did
  not know about. Both worth knowing.
- Some items appear in two phases → the phases overlap; merge or re-cut them.

### Step 4 — Title everything (20 min)

Rewrite each surviving item as a strong, specific title. This is where an outline becomes
writeable.

### Step 5 — Sequence check (15 min)

Read the titles in order as though you knew nothing. At each one ask: **do I have
everything I need to understand this?** If not, something is out of order or missing.

---

## The dependency trap

The most common structural error: teaching something in lesson 6 that lesson 3 assumed.

It happens because you know everything, so you cannot feel the gap. The sequence check
above catches most of it. The rest is caught by giving your outline to someone who does
not know the subject and asking them to flag anything that sounds like it came from
nowhere.

Ten minutes of someone else's time, and it saves a restructure.

---

## Write it down properly

Create every file now, as a stub:

```
03-Editing/
  00-Module-Overview.md
  01-The-Three-Adjustments.md
  02-Getting-Consistency-Across-A-Range.md
  03-Batch-Editing-Without-Losing-Quality.md
```

Each stub contains only its title and a one-line note on what it covers.

Creating the files before writing them does something psychologically useful: the course
now visibly exists, and each session is "fill in a file" rather than "work on the course".
That difference matters more than it should.

---

## Your outline will change. That is fine.

Expect to reorder two or three lessons, merge a couple, and cut one or two once you start
writing. That is the outline doing its job — revealing problems cheaply.

What you should *not* do is add. New ideas go to `Course Two.md`, per your scope
commitment. The outline is allowed to shrink and reshuffle. It is not allowed to grow.

---

## Action Items

1. **Run the 90-minute session.** All five steps, in one sitting if possible.
2. **Create every file as a stub**, numbered, in module folders.
3. **Do the sequence check** by reading titles in order as a beginner would.
4. **Send your outline to one person** who does not know the subject. Ask only: *"is
   there anywhere this seems to skip a step?"*
5. **Count your lessons.** 15–25? If more, cut. If far fewer, check you are not being
   too abstract.
