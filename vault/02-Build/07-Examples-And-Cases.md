# 2.7 — Examples, stories and case studies

**10 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The difference between a good course and a bad one

It is almost never the information. Two courses can contain the same facts and one is
worth £47 while the other is worth nothing.

The difference is **examples**. Abstract advice is unusable; the same advice attached to a
concrete instance is actionable. This is the single highest-leverage editing pass you can
make, and it is the one most first-time writers skip because examples feel like padding.

They are not padding. They are the mechanism by which the reader understands.

---

## Watch what an example does

> **Without:** Make sure your lighting is consistent across your product range.

Fine. True. Completely unusable — the reader does not know what consistent means, what
inconsistent looks like, or how to achieve it.

> **With:** I shot 40 candles over three days. Days one and two were overcast; day three
> was bright. In the listing, the day-three candles look like a different product — same
> wax, same wick, and they read as cheaper. I reshot all 40 in one afternoon behind a net
> curtain and the range finally looked like a range.

Same advice. Now the reader knows what the failure looks like, why it matters
commercially, and what the fix was. They can act on it.

---

## Five kinds of example, in order of value

### 1. Your own mistake

The most valuable thing you can put in a course, and the thing people are most reluctant
to include.

> *"I spent £300 on a lighting kit before realising the problem was that I was shooting at
> 9pm. The kit is still in the box."*

It works for two reasons: it is specific in a way invented examples never are, and it
makes you trustworthy. A course where the author has never been wrong reads as marketing.
A course where they say "I wasted three months on this, here is what I should have done"
reads as true — because it is.

### 2. The before and after

Show the bad version, explain why it fails, show the fix. For anything visual, literally
show both. For anything written, quote both.

### 3. The specific number

Vague: "this takes a while". Specific: "this took me eleven hours over four evenings".

Numbers do disproportionate work for credibility. They must be real ones — see the
warning below.

### 4. The named tool or setting

"Use editing software" tells the reader nothing. "In Lightroom, the three sliders are
Exposure, White Balance and Highlights — in that order, and ignore everything else on the
first pass" is actionable.

Be specific even though specifics date. A slightly out-of-date specific beats a
permanently useless generality, and updating it is a five-minute job.

### 5. The counter-example

Where the advice does not apply. "This works for physical products with texture. For flat
artwork it makes things worse, and here is what to do instead."

Counter-examples build enormous trust because they demonstrate you understand the boundary
of your own advice.

## Case studies

A case study is one example extended: a situation, what was done, what happened.

**Structure:**

1. **The situation** — specific enough to recognise
2. **What was tried first** — and why it did not work
3. **What was actually done** — the steps
4. **The result** — with real numbers
5. **What would be done differently**

Point 5 is the one that makes it credible. A case study with no friction in it reads as
an advertisement.

### The rule on numbers, and it is not optional

**Every number in a case study must be real, and you must be able to evidence it.**

- Your own numbers: fine. Screenshot them, state the period, state that they are yours.
- A customer's numbers: you need their permission to publish AND evidence of the figure.
  Once it is on your page it is your claim, not theirs.
- Invented numbers "for illustration": not acceptable if presented as real. If you must
  illustrate with hypotheticals, say so explicitly — *"suppose you sell 20 units at £30"*
  is clearly hypothetical and completely fine.

This is the same rule as [[01-Mindset/06-Ethics-And-Claims]], and it is the place people
break it most casually, because a case study feels like storytelling rather than a claim.
It is a claim.

## Where to find examples when you have none

You do have them. They are hard to retrieve because your own experience does not feel like
material. Prompts that surface it:

- What did I get wrong when I was learning this?
- What did I waste money on?
- What piece of standard advice turned out to be useless for me?
- What took far longer than expected, and why?
- What was the moment something clicked?
- What do people ask me most often?
- What did I have to be told twice?

Ten minutes with those questions and a blank file will produce more usable material than
an hour of trying to write examples directly.

If you have taught anyone one-to-one, your memory of *where they got stuck* is a goldmine.
Same for `Their Words.md` from [[01-Mindset/04-Finding-Who-Its-For]].

---

## How many

**At least one concrete example per lesson.** Preferably two.

On your editing pass, read each lesson and ask: *would a reader know exactly what to do
after this, or only what to aim for?* If it is the second, you need an example.

Any lesson with no examples at all is almost certainly your weakest, regardless of how
well written it is.

---

## Action Items

1. **Spend ten minutes** on the retrieval prompts above. Dump everything into
   `Examples.md`. No filtering.
2. **Audit every lesson** for examples. Mark any with none.
3. **Add one concrete example** to each marked lesson, drawn from your dump file.
4. **Write one full case study** using the five-part structure, for your strongest
   lesson.
5. **Check every number** you have written. Real and evidenced, or clearly labelled
   hypothetical. No third option.
