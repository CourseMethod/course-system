# 2.4 — The anatomy of a lesson that works

**12 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The structure

Every lesson in this vault uses the same skeleton. Copy it exactly — consistency is worth
more than variety here, because a reader who learns the shape once can navigate every
lesson afterwards without effort.

```markdown
# 2.4 — The lesson title

**12 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The hook

Why this matters, or what goes wrong without it. 2–4 sentences.

---

## The body

The actual teaching. Broken into ## sections with specific headings.

---

## Action Items

1. Do this specific thing.
2. Then this one.

---

**Next:** [[next-lesson|link]]
**Related:** [[other-lesson]] · [[another]]
```

That is it. Six parts, and four of them are two lines each.

---

## The reading time estimate

Put it at the top. It costs you nothing and it changes behaviour: a reader with twenty
minutes free will start a lesson marked "8 minutes" and will not start one of unknown
length.

Estimate at roughly **200 words per minute**, rounded up. A 1,000-word lesson is 5
minutes. Be slightly generous — finishing early feels good, running over feels like a
broken promise.

---

## The hook

The first paragraph decides whether the lesson gets read. It has one job: **make the cost
of not knowing this concrete.**

Four openings that work:

**Name the failure.**
> Most people photograph products on a white sheet under a ceiling light. This is why the
> photos look grey.

**Contradict the expectation.**
> Buying a lamp will not help. The problem is not how much light you have.

**Ask the question they are actually asking.**
> Why do your photos look flat when the product looks fine in person?

**State the cost.**
> This one setting is why your listing photos look amateur, and it takes ten seconds to
> change.

Openings that do not work: "In this lesson, we will explore...", "Lighting is one of the
most important aspects of photography...", any sentence containing "delve" or "unpack".

**Never open with a definition.** Definitions are the least interesting true thing you can
say, and they signal that the lesson will be a lecture.

---

## The body

### Specific headings

Headings are the skeleton a skimming reader sees. Make them carry meaning.

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| ## Overview | ## Why window light beats every lamp |
| ## Step 1 | ## Move the product, not the light |
| ## Considerations | ## When this does not work |
| ## Conclusion | *(delete it)* |

### Short paragraphs

Two to four sentences. On a phone, a six-sentence paragraph is a wall. Most of your
readers are on a phone at least some of the time.

### Tables for comparisons

Any time you find yourself writing "whereas" or "on the other hand" more than once, you
want a table. Comparisons are dramatically easier to read in two columns than in prose.

### Bold for the load-bearing sentence

One or two per section, on the sentence that carries the point. A reader who reads only
the bold text should get the argument. Bolding six things per paragraph achieves nothing.

### Show the wrong version

Nearly always worth doing. "Here is what most people write, here is why it fails, here is
the fix" teaches better than the fix alone, because it attaches to what the reader is
already doing.

---

## What to cut

Ruthlessly, on the editing pass:

- **Throat-clearing.** "Before we dive in, it's worth noting that..." — delete, start at
  the point.
- **Restating the title.** The reader read it four seconds ago.
- **Hedging.** "This might potentially help in some cases" — either it works or say when
  it does not.
- **Conclusions that summarise.** The Action Items do this job better.
- **Motivational filler.** "You've got this!" — the reader is here for information.
- **Anything you included because it felt too short.** Length is not value.

A good rule: after the first draft, delete the first paragraph and the last paragraph of
every section, then check whether anything was lost. Usually not.

---

## Action Items at the end

Covered fully in [[08-Action-Items]], but the principle: **every lesson ends with
something to do.**

Not "reflect on this". A specific, completable action:

- Bad: "Think about your lighting setup."
- Good: "Take one photo by a window and one under your ceiling light. Put them side by
  side. Note which looks more expensive."

Action items are what turn a course into a result, and they are the single biggest
difference between a course people finish and one they abandon.

---

## Length

**600–1,200 words.** That is 3–6 minutes of reading.

Shorter than 600 and it probably belongs inside another lesson. Longer than 1,200 and it
is probably two lessons, or it has padding.

Do not pad to hit a number. A 400-word lesson that does one thing cleanly is better than
a 900-word one with 500 words of preamble. But check whether a short lesson is short
because it is tight, or short because you skipped the examples — that is the more common
cause, and [[07-Examples-And-Cases]] is the fix.

---

## The consistency payoff

Using the same structure everywhere feels mechanical while you write it. To the reader it
is invisible and enormously helpful: they know where the action items are, they know the
time cost before starting, they know how to get back to the module overview.

Variety in structure is a cost you pay and the reader absorbs. Save the creativity for the
content.

---

## Action Items

1. **Copy the skeleton** into a file called `Templates/Lesson-Template.md` in your vault.
2. **Write one complete lesson** using it, start to finish, including action items. Not a
   draft — a finished one.
3. **Read it aloud.** Anywhere you stumble, the sentence is too long. Fix those.
4. **Check the bold test:** read only the bold sentences. Do they carry the argument?
5. **Check the hook:** does your first paragraph make the cost of not knowing this
   concrete? If it defines something, rewrite it.
