# 2.5 — Writing the first draft fast

**12 minutes · Module 2 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The problem this lesson solves

You have an outline and twenty empty files, and you have been "about to start writing" for
four days.

This is not laziness and it is not writer's block. It is that "write lesson 4" is an
ambiguous task with no obvious first move, and ambiguous tasks get deferred. Everything
below is about removing the ambiguity.

---

## Rule one: the first draft is allowed to be bad

Not "should be forgiven for being bad" — **allowed to be, deliberately, as a method.**

You cannot edit a blank page. Every hour spent perfecting paragraph one is an hour not
spent discovering that lesson 4 does not work. Get the whole thing down badly, then fix
it. That order is faster and produces better results than writing carefully once.

Concretely: if you find yourself rereading a sentence for the third time, you are editing,
and editing during drafting is what makes drafting slow.

---

## The talk-it-out method

The single most effective technique here, and the most underused.

**Open the file. Imagine the reader is sitting opposite you and has just asked the
question in the title. Type your answer as if speaking.**

Do not write. Talk, in text.

The difference is dramatic. Compare:

> *Written:* "Lighting is a critical component of product photography and this lesson will
> examine the various approaches available to the practitioner."

> *Talked:* "Your photos look grey. It's not the camera — it's that you're shooting under
> a ceiling light. Move to a window and the problem mostly disappears."

The second is better in every respect and took a third of the time. It is also how you
would actually explain it to a friend, which is exactly the register a course should be in.

If typing does not unlock it, **use voice notes.** Record yourself explaining the lesson
out loud for five minutes, transcribe it, then tidy. The transcript is usually 80% of a
good lesson and contains phrasing you would never have written.

---

## The 25-minute session

Do not sit down to "work on the course". Sit down to write one lesson, with a timer.

1. Open **one** lesson file. Close everything else.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
3. Write badly and continuously until it rings.
4. Stop. Take a break. Do not reread it.

Most lessons take two or three of these. Two sessions an evening, four evenings a week,
and a 20-lesson course drafts in three weeks.

**The critical rule: do not reread within a session.** Rereading is editing. Editing is a
different mode, and switching between them is where the hours vanish.

---

## When you get stuck mid-lesson

Do not stop and stare. Type the blocker in capitals and keep going:

```
The three adjustments are exposure, white balance and TODO WHAT IS THE THIRD ONE

Exposure first, because...
```

`TODO` markers are searchable and you fix them in the editing pass. Stopping to look
something up breaks the flow and the flow is the whole asset.

Same for examples: `EXAMPLE NEEDED HERE` and move on. Filling in examples is a separate,
easier task — see [[07-Examples-And-Cases]].

---

## Write in whatever order you like

There is no requirement to write lesson 1 first. Write whichever lesson you can see most
clearly today.

In fact, **do not write lesson 1 first.** Introductions are the hardest thing to write
before you know what you are introducing, and a bad experience on lesson 1 poisons the
whole project. Write the lesson you are most confident about, get a win, and write the
introduction last when you know exactly what the course contains.

---

## Beating the week-three slump

Somewhere around lesson 8, you will conclude the course is obvious, derivative and
worthless.

This happens to essentially everyone and it is not information about your course. It is
what immersion in a subject feels like: you have now spent weeks with the material and it
has become obvious *to you*. That is exactly the curse of knowledge described in
[[01-Mindset/03-The-Expertise-Gap]], arriving on schedule.

Three things that help:

1. **Reread `Their Words.md`.** Real people asking the exact questions you are answering.
   The most effective antidote available.
2. **Send one lesson to one person** who does not know the subject. Their response
   recalibrates you in about ten minutes.
3. **Keep writing anyway.** The feeling passes and it is not a signal.

---

## Tracking progress visibly

Put a table at the top of `My Course.md`:

| Lesson | Drafted | Edited |
|--------|---------|--------|
| 1.1 | ✅ | ✅ |
| 1.2 | ✅ | |
| 1.3 | | |

Update it every session. It takes five seconds and it converts "working on the course"
into a visible count, which is what sustains momentum through week three.

---

## What "done" means for a first draft

- Every lesson file has words in it
- Every lesson roughly follows the structure from [[04-Anatomy-Of-A-Lesson]]
- `TODO` markers are fine
- Action items may be missing
- Examples may be missing
- It may be badly written throughout

That is a complete first draft. Do not gold-plate anything until all twenty exist,
because the editing pass will change things and polishing before then wastes the polish.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write one lesson tonight** using the talk-it-out method and a 25-minute timer.
2. **Add the progress table** to `My Course.md`.
3. **Schedule your sessions** in a calendar — actual blocks, not intentions. Four
   evenings a week beats one long weekend.
4. **Pick your starting lesson**: the one you are most confident about. Not lesson 1.
5. **Adopt the `TODO` convention** so nothing stops you mid-flow.
