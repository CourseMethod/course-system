# 1.7 — What realistic actually looks like

**9 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Why this lesson exists

Because the gap between what people expect and what happens is the main cause of quitting
— and almost all of that gap is set by marketing they absorbed before they started.

If you expect sales in week two and get none, you conclude the thing does not work and
stop. If you expect nothing until month three, week two feels exactly on schedule and you
keep going. Same reality, opposite outcomes, and the only difference is the expectation
you walked in with.

So here is the shape of it, without the flattering version.

---

## The timeline, honestly

**Weeks 1–2 — Deciding and outlining.**
Feels slow and unproductive. Is neither. Most of the people who never finish quit here,
because there is nothing visible to show for the effort.

**Weeks 2–5 — Writing.**
The bulk of the work. Somewhere in week three you will decide the whole thing is
worthless. This happens to essentially everyone and is not information about your course.
Keep writing.

**Week 5–6 — Setting up the machinery.**
Sales page, checkout, delivery. A weekend if things go well, two if Stripe decides to
verify your identity. Satisfying, because it is finally visible.

**Week 6 — Launch.**
Usually quieter than expected. Friends and existing contacts, if you have any. Possibly
zero sales, and zero sales on launch day is not a verdict.

**Weeks 6–14 — Distribution.**
The long part. You post consistently to very little response. Somewhere in here, if you
keep going, it starts to compound — not dramatically, just a post that does better than
the others, then a couple more.

**Month 4 onwards — Compounding, or not.**
By now you know whether there is demand. Either you have some sales and can improve on
them, or you have a clear signal that something needs to change — the topic, the
audience, the offer, or the distribution. That is useful information and you could not
have got it any faster.

---

## What "success" looks like early

The numbers that people find deflating and should not:

- **First sale:** somewhere between week 3 and month 4. Wide range, and mostly about
  distribution effort, not product quality.
- **First ten sales:** typically a couple of months after the first.
- **First post that does well:** usually somewhere between post 20 and post 60.
- **Point at which it feels like it is working:** month 3 to 6 for most people who get
  there.

And the one people most need to hear: **a large fraction of people who start this never
finish the course**, let alone sell it. Not because it is impossibly hard — because it is
sustained, unglamorous work with no feedback for a long stretch.

Knowing that is not discouragement. It is the actual competitive landscape. The main
thing separating people who succeed from people who do not is finishing.

---

## The four numbers that actually matter

Ignore follower counts and view counts. These four:

**1. Did you finish the course?** Binary. Nothing downstream matters until yes.

**2. Are you publishing consistently?** Not "did one post do well" — are you posting
at a sustainable rate you can hold for three months.

**3. Conversion rate on your page.** Of people who arrive, how many buy. Under 1% means
the page or the traffic is wrong. 1–3% on cold traffic is normal. Above 3% is strong. The
dashboard in this system calculates it for you.

**4. Refund rate.** Above 10% means you are promising something different from what you
deliver. That is a fixable problem and an important signal — see
[[03-Sell/09-Refunds-And-Support]].

---

## Three beliefs that will cost you months

**"I need to build an audience first."**
No. Build the product first, distribute while you write. An audience built before you have
anything to sell is an audience you have trained to expect free content, and converting
them later is harder than starting fresh.

**"It needs to be finished before anyone sees it."**
No. Show drafts to three people at 60% done. The cost of finding out your structure is
wrong at 60% is small. At 100% it is a rewrite.

**"If it doesn't sell immediately, it's a bad idea."**
No. It means nobody has seen it yet. These are entirely different diagnoses with entirely
different remedies, and conflating them is how people abandon perfectly good products.
See [[04-Market/09-When-Nothing-Works]] for how to tell them apart.

---

## What this is genuinely good for

Setting expectations downward is only useful if the upside is real. It is:

- **It compounds.** The course you write once sells for years. The second one takes a
  third of the time because you have the system.
- **It is genuinely yours.** No platform can change its terms and remove your income.
  You own the file, the customer list and the code.
- **It is low-risk.** Total downside is a domain name and your time. There is no
  inventory, no premises, no staff, no debt.
- **The skills transfer.** Writing clearly, explaining things, understanding what people
  actually want — these are useful regardless of what happens to this particular course.

---

## The one-year view

The honest framing: this is a project where most of the value arrives after the point
where most people stop.

Twelve months in, having finished a course and posted consistently, you will have a
product that sells at some rate, a mailing list, a body of content, and a clear sense of
what your market responds to. That is a real asset, built from about a hundred hours of
work and fifteen pounds.

It will not be a screenshot anyone posts on Instagram. It will be better than most of
the ones that are.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write your own timeline** in `My Course.md` with real dates, based on the hours you
   actually have. Not the hours you wish you had.
2. **Decide your quit condition in advance.** For example: *"I will post consistently for
   90 days and complete the course before evaluating whether this works."* Deciding this
   now, while calm, prevents deciding it in week four while discouraged.
3. **Write down what you expect to happen in week 2.** Then read it in week 2. This is
   the cheapest calibration exercise available to you.
4. **Pick your four numbers** and note where you will find each one.
