# 1.5 — Imposter syndrome, practically

**7 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Not a pep talk

You are going to feel like a fraud at several specific points in this process. That
feeling is not a signal that you should stop, and it is also not something you can
think your way out of by reading affirmations.

What follows is the practical version: where the feeling shows up, what it is actually
telling you, and what to do at each point. Treat it as a troubleshooting guide rather
than encouragement.

---

## The four moments

### 1. When you choose the topic

*"Loads of people know this better than me."*

**What it's actually telling you:** that you know enough about the field to be aware of
people ahead of you. Beginners do not have this feeling, because they cannot see the
people ahead. The feeling is evidence of competence, which is a genuinely unhelpful
design flaw in human psychology.

**What to do:** re-read [[03-The-Expertise-Gap]]. Then write down the name of the person
you are actually teaching — not the expert you are comparing yourself to. Are you ahead
of *that* person? That is the only question.

### 2. When you write the first lesson

*"This is obvious. Nobody would pay for this."*

**What it's actually telling you:** that you have internalised the knowledge. Things you
know well feel obvious. This is the curse of knowledge from the inside, and it means you
will systematically under-rate the value of your best material.

**What to do:** show one lesson to someone who does not know the subject. Watch where
they slow down. The parts that felt too obvious to include are usually the parts they
needed most. Do this once and the feeling loses most of its power, because you will have
direct evidence against it.

### 3. When you set the price

*"I can't charge for this."*

**What it's actually telling you:** that you are pricing your *time to produce* rather
than the buyer's *value received*. Those are unrelated numbers. Four hours of your writing
that saves someone three months is not worth four hours of your hourly rate.

**What to do:** see [[03-Sell/01-Pricing]]. Also note: charging nothing does not help the
customer. Free courses have completion rates in the single digits. Paying is part of what
makes people actually do the thing.

### 4. When you publish

*"Someone is going to call me out."*

**What it's actually telling you:** usually nothing. Occasionally something real — see
below.

**What to do:** check whether you have made any claim you cannot support
([[06-Ethics-And-Claims]]). If you have not, publish. If you have, fix that claim, then
publish. The fear is only actionable in the second case.

---

## The version of this feeling you should listen to

Not all of it is noise. There is a real signal buried in there, and it sounds different:

| Noise | Signal |
|-------|--------|
| "Someone knows this better than me" | "I have never actually done this" |
| "This feels too simple" | "I made this up and have not tested it" |
| "Who am I to charge?" | "I am claiming a result I cannot support" |
| "I'm not an expert" | "I would have to research every lesson to write it" |

The left column is imposter syndrome and should be overridden. The right column is your
judgement working correctly and should be acted on — by changing the claim, changing the
scope, or changing the topic.

The distinguishing question: **is the discomfort about my standing, or about something
specific I have written?** Standing is noise. A specific claim is signal.

---

## The thing that actually dissolves it

Not confidence. Evidence.

One person telling you a specific lesson helped them does more than any amount of
internal reassurance, and it is the reason [[02-Build/09-Packaging-And-Shipping]]
recommends giving your draft to three people before launch. Not for feedback on quality —
for the evidence.

Until then, use the substitute: **write down what you are NOT claiming.** Explicitly.

> I am not claiming to be the best at this.
> I am not claiming this is the only way.
> I am not claiming a specific result for anyone.
> I am claiming: this is what I did, in order, and here is what I would skip.

That fourth line is completely defensible, entirely honest, and genuinely valuable. Once
it is written down, most of the anxiety has nowhere to attach itself — you are no longer
defending a position you never held.

---

## A note on the opposite problem

Some people have none of this feeling, and that is worse.

If you are entirely comfortable claiming expertise you do not have, quoting results you
have not achieved, and promising outcomes you cannot influence — that is the failure mode
on the other side, and it is the one that produces refund demands, chargebacks, closed
payment accounts and, in the case of earnings claims, regulatory attention.

A moderate amount of "am I sure about this?" is a functioning quality-control mechanism.
The goal is to calibrate it, not remove it.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write the "not claiming" list** in `My Course.md`. Four lines, as above. You will
   reuse it almost verbatim on your sales page.
2. **Identify which of the four moments** you are in right now, and apply the specific
   remedy for it rather than trying to feel better generally.
3. **Sort your current discomfort** into the noise/signal table. If anything lands in the
   right column, deal with it now — that is your judgement doing its job.
4. **Line up three people** who will read a draft. Ask them now, while it costs nothing.
   You will need them at [[02-Build/09-Packaging-And-Shipping]].
