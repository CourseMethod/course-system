# 1.3 — The expertise gap

**8 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The question everyone gets stuck on

*"Who am I to teach this?"*

It is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, because the instinct behind it is
sound: there genuinely are people who teach things they have no business teaching, and
you are right not to want to be one of them.

But the question is usually being asked against the wrong benchmark.

---

## You are not competing with the expert

The mental image is: you, versus the person who has done this for twenty years and wrote
the definitive book. Against that comparison you lose, obviously, and so you conclude you
have nothing to offer.

That is not the comparison. The actual comparison is:

**You, versus the confused beginner's current situation.**

Their current situation is: seventeen browser tabs, three contradictory YouTube videos,
a Reddit thread from 2019, and no idea which order to do things in. That is your
competition. You beat it by being clear and specific.

---

## Why the twenty-year expert is often a worse teacher

This is not a consolation prize. It is a real, documented phenomenon — sometimes called
the curse of knowledge, and it has a specific mechanism.

When you learn a skill deeply, the individual steps compress into single automatic
actions. The expert driver does not think "mirror, signal, manoeuvre" — they just change
lane. This compression is what makes expertise fast, and it makes the component steps
genuinely invisible to the person performing them.

So when the expert teaches, they skip steps. Not carelessly — they cannot see the steps
any more. The beginner hears "then you just set up your environment" and has no idea what
that means, and the expert cannot understand why.

**You, two years in, still have access to the steps.** You remember that the confusing
part was not the concept but the fact that the menu item had moved. You remember which
piece of advice everyone repeats that turned out to be wrong. That memory is worth more
than the extra eighteen years of expertise, and it has a short shelf life — you will lose
it too, which is an argument for writing this down sooner rather than later.

---

## The "one chapter ahead" standard

Here is the standard that actually applies:

> **You need to be far enough ahead that the reader cannot see the edge of your
> knowledge from where they are standing — and honest about where that edge is.**

Both halves matter.

Someone two weeks into a skill cannot teach it, because the beginner will immediately hit
questions they cannot answer. Six months to two years ahead is usually plenty, provided
you scope the course to what you actually know.

And the second half — honesty about the edge — is what separates a legitimate course from
a fraudulent one. "Here is how I did this, here is what I would skip if I started again,
and here is where my experience runs out" is a genuinely valuable and completely honest
product. "I am a leading authority" when you are not is where people get into trouble,
both reputationally and legally. See [[06-Ethics-And-Claims]].

---

## What you are actually selling

Not information. Information is free and your customer already has too much of it.

You are selling three things:

**1. Sequence.** The order to do things in. This is the single most valuable thing you
have and the thing beginners most lack. They can find every individual fact; what they
cannot find is what to do first, second and third, and what to ignore entirely.

**2. Compression.** You spent six months finding out that three of the five recommended
approaches are dead ends. Telling them that saves four months. That saving is the product.

**3. Confidence that it works.** "This worked for me, here is exactly what I did" is
worth more than "here are seven possible approaches". People are not short of options.
They are short of a reason to commit to one.

None of those requires you to be the world's foremost anything.

---

## A sanity check

You are qualified to teach X if you can answer yes to most of these:

- Could you sit with a beginner for an hour and genuinely help them?
- Has anyone asked you about this without you prompting them?
- Do you know at least three mistakes beginners make that you no longer make?
- Could you explain why the obvious approach is wrong, if it is?
- Do you know what you *don't* know about it, well enough to say so?

That last one is the real credential. Knowing the boundary of your knowledge is the thing
that lets you teach inside it honestly.

---

## When you genuinely are not qualified

To be clear, because "everyone is qualified" would be a lie:

- You have never actually done the thing, only read about it
- Your experience is a single lucky outcome you could not repeat or explain
- The subject has real consequences if taught wrong — medical, legal, financial advice,
  anything load-bearing for someone's safety — and you have no standing in it
- You would have to research every lesson from scratch to write it

That last one is the practical test. If writing the course means learning the subject,
you are not teaching, you are studying in public and charging for it.

In those cases, the honest move is to pick something else from your list in
[[02-Picking-Your-Topic]], or go and get the experience first.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write your qualification sentence** in `My Course.md`:
   > *I know this because [specific experience]. My experience runs out at [boundary].*
   Both halves. The second one is the honest bit and it will go on your sales page.
2. **List three mistakes** beginners make in your topic that you no longer make. If you
   cannot, you may be less far ahead than you think — worth knowing now.
3. **Write down the thing that confused you most** when you were learning this, and what
   finally made it click. That is very likely your strongest lesson, and possibly your
   hook — see [[04-Market/03-Hooks]].
