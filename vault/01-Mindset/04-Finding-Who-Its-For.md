# 1.4 — Finding who it's for

**10 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Why this is a separate lesson from picking the topic

Because "who it's for" is not the same question as "what it's about", and conflating them
produces courses that teach a subject rather than helping a person.

A course *about* Excel is a reference manual. A course for *someone who has inherited a
broken spreadsheet they are now responsible for and cannot ask about without looking
incompetent* is a product. Same subject. Entirely different thing to write, and only one
of them has an obvious buyer.

---

## The specific person

You are going to write for one person. Not a demographic, not an "avatar" with a stock
photo and an invented name — a real situation, described precisely enough that you could
recognise it.

The format that works:

> **Someone who [situation], has tried [what they tried], and is stuck because [obstacle].
> They want [outcome] but are worried about [fear].**

Worked examples:

> Someone who **runs a two-person landscaping business**, has tried **posting on Facebook
> occasionally**, and is stuck because **they have no idea why some posts get calls and
> others get nothing**. They want **a steady stream of local enquiries** but are worried
> about **wasting money on ads that do nothing**.

> Someone who **learned Spanish to a decent level**, has tried **Duolingo for Portuguese**,
> and is stuck because **the similarity keeps tripping them up**. They want **to have a
> real conversation in Portuguese within three months** but are worried about **embedding
> Spanish habits that will be hard to unlearn**.

Notice how much writing guidance is contained in those. You now know what to include,
what to skip, what tone to use, and what objection to address on the sales page.

---

## Where to find the real version

Do not invent this. Go and look at where these people actually talk:

**Reddit.** Find the subreddit for your topic and sort by new. Read the questions people
ask. Read the phrasing — the exact words they use are the words your sales page should
use, and they are almost never the words an expert would use.

**YouTube comments.** Under the most popular tutorial in your area. The comments saying
"this was great but I still don't understand..." are a list of gaps in the existing
material, which is to say a list of lessons you should write.

**Facebook groups and Discord servers** for your niche. Search within them for "how do I"
and read a hundred results.

**Your own inbox and DMs.** If people already ask you things, that archive is the most
valuable research available to you and you already own it.

**Amazon reviews of the books in your area.** Specifically the three-star reviews. Five
stars tell you nothing; one star is usually about shipping. Three-star reviews are
written by people who engaged seriously and found something missing, and that something
is your opportunity.

Spend thirty minutes on this. Collect actual sentences, copied verbatim, into a note
called `Their Words.md`.

---

## The mistake: writing for who you were

The natural instinct is to write for yourself-two-years-ago. It is a good starting point
and it has one systematic flaw: you are not typical.

You had particular advantages — maybe technical background, maybe time, maybe an unusual
starting point — and you will unconsciously assume them. The landscaper does not have
your comfort with software. The person learning Portuguese may not have your ear for
grammar.

So: start from yourself-two-years-ago, then go and check against real strangers. The gap
between the two is where courses fail.

---

## How narrow is too narrow?

Almost never an actual problem, but here is the boundary.

Too narrow is when the person you have described **does not know they are that person**.
If your audience is "people who use the 2019 version of a specific plugin on Tuesdays",
nobody self-identifies that way and nobody searches for it.

Practical test: **would your reader say the description out loud about themselves?**

- "I'm a freelancer who's bad at invoicing" — yes, people say this
- "I'm a mid-funnel B2B SaaS marketing operator" — nobody says this
- "I want to get back to running without wrecking my knees again" — yes
- "I am a 35-49 fitness-curious demographic" — no

If they would say it, it is specific, not too narrow.

---

## Two audiences you should avoid on your first course

**"Complete beginners" in a large field.** Enormous, cheap, high-support, and they
usually do not yet know what they need. "Learn photography from scratch" competes with
every free resource on earth. "Product photography for Etsy sellers using a phone"
competes with almost nothing.

**Other people who want to sell courses**, unless you have actually sold courses. This is
the loop discussed in [[01-Why-This-Works]], and it is where the category's bad
reputation comes from. If you have not done the thing, teaching people to teach the thing
is not a business, it is a chain letter.

---

## What this gives you later

Once you have this written down, a lot of downstream decisions become easy rather than
agonising:

| Later decision | Answered by |
|----------------|-------------|
| What goes in the outline? | What this person needs, in order |
| What tone? | How this person talks |
| What price? | What this outcome is worth to them ([[03-Sell/01-Pricing]]) |
| What headline? | Their situation, named back to them ([[03-Sell/04-Writing-Copy]]) |
| What to post about? | Their questions ([[04-Market/03-Hooks]]) |
| Which objections to handle? | Their fear ([[03-Sell/03-The-Sales-Page]]) |

This is why the ten minutes is worth it. Every one of those decisions gets made
eventually. Making them from a clear picture is dramatically faster than making them
from nothing.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write the paragraph** in `My Course.md` using the format above. All five blanks.
2. **Spend 30 minutes** in the places listed. Collect at least 15 verbatim sentences into
   `Their Words.md`. Copy exactly — do not tidy the grammar, the phrasing is the point.
3. **Find the three-star reviews** of the two best-known books or courses in your area.
   Write down what people say is missing. That list is a draft of your table of contents.
4. **Check the narrowness test.** Would your person say your description about themselves?
   If not, rewrite it in their words.
