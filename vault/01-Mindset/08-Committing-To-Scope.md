# 1.8 — Committing to a scope you'll finish

**8 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The last decision before you build

You have a topic ([[02-Picking-Your-Topic]]) and a person ([[04-Finding-Who-Its-For]]).
One thing left: **how big is this?**

Get it wrong in the obvious direction and you produce something thin that refunds badly.
Get it wrong in the far more common direction and you produce nothing at all, because
the scope grew faster than you could write.

---

## Scope creep is the main killer

Watch how it happens. It is never one bad decision:

> *"I'll teach people to photograph their Etsy products with a phone."*
> — sensible, tight, writeable in three weeks
>
> *"...but I should probably cover lighting properly."*
> — reasonable
>
> *"...and editing, otherwise the photos still look bad."*
> — also reasonable
>
> *"...and I can't really skip how Etsy's listing algorithm works."*
> — hmm
>
> *"...and pricing, since good photos won't help if the price is wrong."*
> — and now it is a book

Each step is individually defensible. Collectively they have turned a three-week project
into a six-month one, and six-month projects undertaken in evenings mostly do not finish.

**The mechanism is that every addition feels like generosity.** It is not. An unfinished
course helps nobody, and a course that tries to cover everything teaches nothing well.

---

## The right size for a first course

**15 to 25 lessons. 600–1,200 words each. One clear outcome.**

That is roughly 15,000–25,000 words — a long weekend's reading for the customer, three to
five weeks of writing for you. It is enough to be substantial and charge properly for, and
small enough to finish.

Signs you have scoped correctly:

- You can list every lesson title without research
- You could explain the whole course to someone in two minutes
- There is exactly one outcome, and you can name it in a sentence
- You are slightly worried it is too small

That last one is the reliable indicator. Almost everyone's correctly-scoped first course
feels too small to them, because they are measuring against everything they know rather
than against what the reader needs.

---

## The one-outcome test

Write this sentence:

> **By the end of this course, you will be able to ________.**

Rules: one blank, one verb, no "and".

| Fails the test | Passes |
|----------------|--------|
| "...understand photography and grow your Etsy shop" | "...photograph your products so they look professional" |
| "...learn Git and work in a team and deploy safely" | "...use Git without fear of losing work" |
| "...get fit and eat better and sleep well" | "...run 5k without hurting your knees" |

If you need "and", you have two courses. Write one now, and the other one second — it
will take a third of the time, and you will have customers for it already.

---

## What to do with everything you cut

Do not throw it away. Put it in a note called `Course Two.md`.

This does two things. It stops the cut material nagging at you, because it is safely
recorded rather than lost. And it gives you a genuine head start on your second product,
which is where the compounding in this business actually happens
([[05-Bonus/01-Your-Second-Product]]).

Cut material is also your best free content. Every deleted section is a post, and it is
material you have already thought through ([[04-Market/05-Content-Calendar]]).

---

## Write the contract with yourself

Before you open Module 2, write this in `My Course.md` and be specific:

```
SCOPE COMMITMENT

Course title:        ______________________
One outcome:         By the end, they will be able to ______
Number of lessons:   ____ (aim 15–25)
Target word count:   ____ (aim 15,000–25,000)
Draft deadline:      ____ (a real date)

Out of scope — going in Course Two.md:
  - ______________________
  - ______________________
  - ______________________

I will not add lessons before the first draft is complete.
If I think of something, it goes in Course Two.md.
```

The last two lines are the operative ones. Everything else is bookkeeping.

---

## Permission to be imperfect

Your first course will not be your best work. This is not a risk to manage, it is a
certainty to accept, and accepting it early is what lets you finish.

What matters is that it is **honest and useful**, not that it is comprehensive. A tight
course that does one thing well, with a clear statement of what it does not cover, is a
good product. Buyers are not disappointed by a narrow scope they were told about. They
are disappointed by a broad promise delivered thinly.

You will update it. That is what lifetime updates are for — and it is why offering them
costs you nothing and reassures the buyer considerably.

---

## When to break these rules

Scope up if:

- You have sold a course before and know you finish things
- The outcome genuinely cannot be reached in 20 lessons (rare, and worth double-checking)
- You are writing full-time rather than in evenings

Scope down if:

- You have under four hours a week
- You have started and abandoned projects before
- You are not sure you can list 20 lesson titles

When in doubt, scope down. A finished small course beats an unfinished large one by an
infinite margin, and the small one teaches you the entire system.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write the one-outcome sentence.** One verb, no "and". Rewrite until it passes.
2. **List your lesson titles.** Just titles. If you have more than 25, cut to 25 and move
   the rest to `Course Two.md`.
3. **Fill in the scope commitment** above, with a real draft deadline.
4. **Create `Course Two.md`** now, with the cut material in it. Having the destination
   ready is what makes cutting painless later.
