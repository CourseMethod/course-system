# 1.2 — Picking your topic in 12 minutes

**12 minutes · Module 1 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

This is the most important lesson in the vault. Not because choosing well is hard —
because leaving the choice *open* is what kills the project.

People do not fail here by picking the wrong topic. They fail by picking a topic
provisionally, starting work, finding the work difficult, concluding that the difficulty
is evidence the topic was wrong, and switching. Then repeating. Six weeks later they have
three half-outlines and no course.

**The work is difficult because writing is difficult, not because your topic is wrong.**

So: twelve minutes, a decision, and then the decision is closed.

---

## The only two criteria that matter

Ignore passion. Ignore market size. Ignore whether you are "the right person". Two things:

### 1. You are meaningfully ahead of someone

Not the best in the world. Ahead of the person you are teaching, and able to remember
being where they are.

Two years ahead is often better than twenty. The twenty-year expert has forgotten which
parts were confusing. They skip steps without noticing because those steps became
automatic a decade ago. You, two years in, still remember the specific thing that made no
sense in week three.

### 2. Someone has already tried to pay for the answer

Not "would this sell?" — a question nobody can answer in advance. Instead: **has anyone
ever asked you about this, or paid someone for it?**

Evidence that counts:
- People ask you how you did it
- You have been paid to do it, even once, even badly
- You searched for a guide to it yourself and could not find a good one
- There is a paid product for it that is out of date or poorly made

That last one is the strongest signal, and people consistently misread it as bad news.
Existing competition means someone has already proven people pay. An empty market usually
means there is no market.

---

## The 12-minute exercise

Get a timer. Actually do this — reading about it does nothing.

### Minutes 1–4: Dump

In `My Course.md`, list everything you have done for more than six months. No filtering.
Include:

- Jobs and roles, including unglamorous ones
- Software and tools you know well
- Hobbies you have got properly good at
- Problems you have solved for yourself
- Things people ask you about
- Things you learned the hard way

Aim for fifteen items. Most people stop at six because they are filtering while writing.
Do not filter. "Made a spreadsheet that tracks my freelance invoices" belongs on the list.

### Minutes 5–8: Narrow

Take the five most promising and make each one specific. The pattern:

> **[Specific skill]** for **[specific person]** who wants **[specific outcome]**

Watch what specificity does:

| Too broad | Specific enough |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Photography | Product photography for Etsy sellers using only a phone |
| Fitness | Getting back to running after 40 without injuring yourself |
| Excel | Excel for people who inherited someone else's broken spreadsheet |
| Marketing | Local SEO for single-location restaurants |
| Coding | Git for designers who keep breaking things and don't know why |

The right-hand column looks smaller. It is not — it is *findable*. Somebody types the
right-hand column into a search box. Nobody types "photography".

Narrow also makes writing far easier, because you know exactly who you are talking to.

### Minutes 9–11: Score

For each of your five, score 1–5 honestly:

| Question | Score |
|----------|-------|
| How far ahead of a beginner am I? | /5 |
| Has anyone actually asked me about this? | /5 |
| Could I list 20 things to teach without research? | /5 |
| Would I still be willing to work on this in two months? | /5 |

That third question is the practical one and it does most of the work. If you cannot
brainstorm twenty sub-topics off the top of your head, you do not know it well enough
yet, and you will stall in Module 2 when the outline needs filling.

### Minute 12: Decide

Highest total wins. Write it in `My Course.md` as:

> **I am teaching [who] how to [do what], so they can [get what result].**

Then — this is the part that matters — add:

> **I am not reconsidering this until I have finished a first draft.**

---

## If two are tied

Pick the one you could start writing *tonight*. Enthusiasm fades; a lower barrier to the
first sentence does not.

## If nothing scores above 12

You are being too modest, which is the normal failure mode here — go back to your dump
list and look at the items you dismissed instantly. Ask someone who knows you what they
would come to you for; the answer is often obvious to everyone but you.

If you genuinely have nothing, the honest answer is to go and get six months of real
experience at something. That is a real answer, not a brush-off. This system will still
be here.

---

## The objection you are having right now

> *"But loads of people already teach this."*

Good. See [[01-Why-This-Works]] — competition is proof of a market, and most of it is
general where you can be specific.

> *"But I'm not the best at it."*

Irrelevant. See [[03-The-Expertise-Gap]], which is the next lesson and exists entirely to
address this.

> *"But what if I pick wrong?"*

Then you will have learned the entire system — writing, pricing, selling, distribution —
and your second course will take a third of the time. A wrong first course is a far
better outcome than a perfect outline you never ship. There is no version of this where
deliberating longer helps.

---

## Action Items

1. **Do the 12-minute exercise.** Timer on. Now, not later.
2. **Write the sentence** in `My Course.md`, including the "not reconsidering" line.
3. **Tell one person** what you have chosen. Saying it out loud makes reversing it
   slightly costly, which is exactly the friction you want.
4. **List 20 sub-topics** you could teach within it. If you struggle past 12, that is a
   signal worth heeding — reconsider *now*, before you have invested weeks.
