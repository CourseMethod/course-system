# 3.3 — The sales page, section by section

**16 minutes · Module 3 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## What a sales page is actually doing

It is answering, in order, the questions a sceptical stranger has:

1. What is this?
2. Is it for me?
3. What exactly do I get?
4. Why should I believe you?
5. What does it cost?
6. What if it's wrong for me?
7. What happens when I click?

Every section below exists to answer one of those. If a section on your page answers none
of them, cut it.

**A reader who leaves does so at the first unanswered question.** That is the whole
diagnostic framework — when a page underperforms, find which question it fails to answer
and answer it.

---

## The structure

This is the structure of the page in this system, and you can read its source. Fourteen
sections, in this order.

### 1. Headline

One job: **name the reader's situation more precisely than they expect.**

Not what you sell. What is true about them.

> Weak: "The Complete Product Photography Course"
> Strong: "Your products look better in person than in your photos. That's fixable."

Specificity is the hook. A vague promise reads as noise; a precise observation reads as
competence and earns the next ten seconds.

### 2. Sub-headline

What it is, plainly, in one sentence. The headline hooks; this one orients.

> "A 20-lesson course on shooting listing photos with a phone and a window."

### 3. Primary call to action, immediately

Above the fold. Some readers arrive already convinced — usually returning visitors or
people sent by a recommendation — and making them scroll past your whole argument is a
tax on your warmest traffic.

### 4. Trust line

Three short facts under the button. One payment, refund policy, no subscription. Removes
the cheapest objections before they form.

### 5. The problem

Describe their situation accurately enough that they feel understood.

This is where hype-driven pages go wrong: they manufacture pain. You do not need to. Just
describe the actual situation precisely, and the reader does the rest — *"then it stops,
at one of four places"* is more persuasive than any amount of "imagine finally being
free".

### 6. What this actually is

Concrete and specific. The single strongest trust-builder available to a seller with no
testimonials is describing the product so precisely that it is obvious you built it.

File format, lesson count, how it opens, what it does not include.

### 7. What's inside

**Show the full contents.** Every lesson title, not a teaser.

This feels like giving away too much. It is not — the titles are not the course, and a
buyer who can see exactly what they get converts far better than one asked to trust a
summary. It also filters out people for whom it is wrong, which lowers your refund rate.

### 8. Who it's for and who it isn't

The "not for you" column is the highest-trust section on the page.

Telling people not to buy is the most efficient credibility mechanism available, and it
genuinely reduces refunds. Someone who reads "this doesn't fit if you need money this
month" and buys anyway has self-selected accurately.

### 9. Proof

Real testimonials if you have them ([[05-Bonus/04-Collecting-Testimonials]]). If you do
not, **say so and point at the proof that does exist** — the inspectable contents, the
guarantee, the fact that they can see the product before paying.

Do not invent testimonials. See [[01-Mindset/06-Ethics-And-Claims]]; it is illegal, it is
spotted, and the honest empty state converts better than quotes the reader disbelieves.

### 10. Pricing

Cards, then a comparison table. Then a short paragraph telling them which to pick and why.

### 11. The guarantee

Its own section, not a footnote. See [[09-Refunds-And-Support]] for the wording that
works and why conditional guarantees backfire.

### 12. FAQ

Objection handling. The most important section on the page after the headline — full
treatment below.

### 13. Final CTA

Short. One line restating the core idea, one button.

### 14. Footer with the boring necessities

Support contact, refund request, privacy, terms, and your results disclaimer if you have
made any claim at all.

## The FAQ is where sales are won

Most pages waste it on logistics. The FAQ should handle the **real** objections — the
ones people think and do not say.

For every objection, answer the question underneath the question:

| They ask | They mean | Answer that works |
|----------|-----------|-------------------|
| "How long is it?" | "Will I finish it?" | Length, plus how it is structured to be done in pieces |
| "Is this for beginners?" | "Will I be lost?" | Exactly what you assume they know |
| "What makes you qualified?" | "Are you a fraud?" | Your specific experience, and where it ends |
| "Why so cheap?" | "What's wrong with it?" | The honest structural reason |
| "Can't I find this free?" | "Am I being ripped off?" | Yes, in pieces, over months. You're buying the sequence |

### The rules for FAQ answers

**Answer honestly, including when the honest answer is not flattering.** "Yes, you could
find most of this free across forty sources over three months" is a better answer than
pretending otherwise, because the reader already knows it and your denial would cost you
the sale.

**Address the strongest version of the objection.** Answering a weak version is
transparent and makes the reader trust you less.

**Do not be defensive.** "Isn't this just a course about selling courses?" deserves a
direct answer, not a deflection.

---

## Length

Longer than you think, but every section must earn its place.

A serious purchase decision needs information. What kills long pages is not length but
**repetition** — the same claim restated four times in different fonts, which is what
"long-form sales page" has come to mean and why people scroll past them.

Rule: **every section answers a different question.** If two sections answer the same
one, merge them.

---

## Design that helps rather than decorates

- **One accent colour.** Everything else neutral.
- **Generous whitespace.** Cramped pages read as cheap.
- **Real hierarchy.** A reader should be able to skim only the headings and get the
  argument.
- **Readable body text.** 16–18px, comfortable line length. Most of your traffic is on a
  phone.
- **Buttons that look like buttons.**
- **No stock photos of people in offices.** They signal "template" instantly.
- **No autoplay video, no popups, no exit-intent overlay.** Each one costs you more in
  irritation than it gains.

---

## Action Items

1. **Write your headline.** Ten versions. Pick the most specific, not the most exciting.
2. **List your reader's seven questions** and check your page answers each one, in order.
3. **Write the "not for you" list.** Five honest entries. Do not soften them.
4. **Write six FAQ answers** using the question-underneath-the-question table.
5. **Put your full lesson list on the page.** All of it.
6. **Read the page aloud.** Anywhere it sounds like an advert rather than a person, rewrite
   it — see [[04-Writing-Copy]].
