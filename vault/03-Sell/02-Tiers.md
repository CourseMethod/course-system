# 3.2 — Tiers, and when a second one helps

**9 minutes · Module 3 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Should you have tiers at all?

Not necessarily. A single price is simpler to build, simpler to explain, and simpler to
decide on — and decision friction is a real cost.

**Add a second tier when you have something genuinely different to sell**, not to create
an illusion of choice. A second tier that is the same course with a bonus PDF is
transparent and slightly insulting.

---

## What actually makes a legitimate second tier

Something that costs you more to deliver, or that a different kind of buyer needs:

- **Your time.** Support, a call, feedback on their work. This is the strongest second
  tier and the one with real scarcity.
- **Additional work product.** Templates, code, a toolkit — something you built that has
  standalone value.
- **A licence difference.** Personal use vs. using it with clients or a team.

What does not make a legitimate tier:

- Artificially removing something from the base tier to create the gap
- "Priority" support when you answer everything within a day anyway
- A "community" that is an empty Discord

---

## The two-tier structure that works

```
Tier 1 — the course             £47
  Everything they need to get the outcome themselves

Tier 2 — the course + your time or your tools    £97–149
  Everything above, plus the thing that costs you something
```

Roughly **2–3× the base price** for the second tier. Much closer and the upgrade feels
pointless; much further and it is a different product requiring a different sales
argument.

Expect around **20–30% of buyers to take the higher tier** if the gap is genuine. If
almost nobody does, the difference is not valuable enough. If almost everybody does, your
base tier is missing something it should have.

---

## The decoy effect, and whether to use it

A well-documented finding: adding a third, deliberately unattractive option makes the
middle option more appealing.

It works. It is also mild manipulation, and you should decide deliberately whether you are
comfortable with it.

The version that is clearly fine: three tiers where all three are real, honestly
described, and the middle genuinely is the best value for most people. Saying so plainly
— *"most people should pick this one"* — is helpful, not manipulative.

The version that is not: inventing a bad tier nobody should buy, purely to make another
look better. That is engineering a choice architecture to mislead.

**For a first product, two honest tiers.** It is enough.

---

## Naming tiers

Avoid Bronze/Silver/Gold — it is generic and implies the cheaper one is deficient.

Name them for **who they are for** or **what they include**:

| Weak | Better |
|------|--------|
| Basic / Pro | The Course / The Course + Coaching |
| Silver / Gold | Self-paced / With feedback |
| Standard / Premium | For yourself / For your team |
| Lite / Full | The Method / The Engine |

The right-hand versions tell the reader which one they are, which is the actual job.

---

## Order and emphasis on the page

- **Cheaper on the left, expensive on the right** in most markets. People read left to
  right and anchor on the first number.
- **Highlight one**, and be honest about why. "Most people want this" is a real
  recommendation, and readers appreciate being told rather than made to work it out.
- **Show a comparison table** below the cards. It answers the "what exactly is the
  difference?" question that otherwise causes people to leave and think about it.

---

## Upgrades

If someone buys the lower tier and later wants the higher one, **charge the difference,
not the full price.**

This costs you a little revenue and buys a lot of goodwill, and it removes the single
biggest reason people hesitate at the cheaper tier: fear of choosing wrong. Say so on the
page — *"start with The Method and upgrade later if you want; you'll pay the difference,
not the full price again"* — and watch the lower tier's conversion improve.

The upsell email in this system's sequence ([[08-Email-Sequence]]) is built around exactly
this.

---

## A tier you should not build yet

**A subscription.** Monthly recurring revenue is attractive and it comes with an
obligation: you must keep producing, forever, or churn destroys it. That is a serious
commitment for someone who has not yet sold a single course.

Sell the one-time product. Consider recurring when you have evidence people want more from
you — see [[05-Bonus/01-Your-Second-Product]].

---

## Action Items

1. **Decide: one tier or two?** If two, write down what the second one genuinely contains
   that costs you more.
2. **Check it against the "not legitimate" list.** If your second tier only exists because
   you removed something from the first, restructure.
3. **Name both tiers** for who they are for, not by metal.
4. **Set the higher price** at 2–3× the base.
5. **Write your upgrade policy** — pay the difference — and put it on the page.
