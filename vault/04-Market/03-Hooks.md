# 4.3 — Hooks that earn the next three seconds

**12 minutes · Module 4 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## Why this is the whole game

Watch time is the dominant ranking signal ([[01-Distribution-From-Zero]]). If people
scroll past in two seconds, nothing else about your post matters — not the editing, not
the information, not the call to action.

**The first three seconds determine whether anything else gets seen.**

This feels like a depressing thing to optimise for. It is really just the same problem as
a book's first page or an email's subject line, compressed. The work is the same: give the
reader a reason to continue.

---

## What a hook has to do

One thing: **create a gap the viewer wants closed.**

Either they need to know the answer, or they disagree and need to see whether you can
defend it, or they recognise themselves and want to know what happens next.

What it must not be: a preamble. "Hi everyone, in today's video I'm going to be talking
about..." has closed no gap by second three, and they are gone.

---

## Eight hook patterns that work

### 1. The specific mistake
> "Your product photos look grey. It's not your camera."

Names something true about them, then removes the explanation they had. They need the
real one.

### 2. The contradiction
> "Stop buying lighting equipment. It won't fix this."

Contradicts the obvious action. Disagreement is engagement — they stay to see if you are
wrong.

### 3. The cost
> "This one setting cost me three months of bad photos."

Stakes. And "three months" is specific, which makes it credible.

### 4. The before and after
> "This is the same candle. Same phone. Ten seconds apart."

Visual proof of a gap. Works exceptionally well because it is undeniable in the first
frame.

### 5. The question they are asking
> "Why do your photos look fine on your phone and terrible on Etsy?"

If it is their actual question, they stop. Comes straight from `Their Words.md`
([[01-Mindset/04-Finding-Who-Its-For]]).

### 6. The number
> "Four things make a product photo look amateur. Here's the first."

Structure promises a payoff and implies brevity. "Here's the first" is doing real work —
it promises the answer starts immediately.

### 7. The confession
> "I spent £300 on a lighting kit. It's still in the box."

Vulnerability and specificity. Very hard to scroll past, and it makes you trustworthy
rather than salesy.

### 8. The direct address
> "If you sell candles on Etsy, this is why your photos aren't converting."

Filters hard and grips the right people completely. Narrow is fine — you want the right
300, not any 3,000.

---

## Hooks that do not work

| Don't | Why |
|-------|-----|
| "Hi everyone, welcome back" | Three seconds spent on nothing |
| "In this video I'll show you..." | Describing the video instead of starting it |
| "Let's talk about lighting" | No gap created |
| "Lighting is so important" | Abstract, and they already suspect this |
| "You won't BELIEVE this" | Clickbait register — recognised and discounted |
| "Follow for more tips" | Asking before giving |

The unifying failure: **talking about the topic instead of starting inside it.**

---

## Write the hook first

Not last. The hook determines the post.

If you cannot write a hook for an idea, the idea is not ready — usually because it is too
abstract. "Lighting" has no hook. "Why your ceiling light is ruining your photos" has one,
and it also tells you exactly what the post contains.

Practical method: **write ten hooks before making anything.** Nine will be weak. That is
the expected ratio, and it is far cheaper to discard a sentence than a finished video.

---

## Say it and show it

On video, the hook needs to work in three modes simultaneously, because you do not know
how it will be consumed:

1. **Spoken** — for people with sound on
2. **On-screen text** — most people watch muted
3. **Visually** — the first frame itself

Best case all three carry it. A before/after where the first frame shows the contrast, the
text says "same candle, ten seconds apart", and you say it too.

**Do not skip the on-screen text.** A substantial majority of short-form viewing is muted,
and a hook that only exists in audio reaches a fraction of its potential.

---

## Where hooks come from

You already have a list, from Module 1 and 2:

- **`Their Words.md`** — real questions in real phrasing. Almost every one is a hook.
- **Your own mistakes** ([[02-Build/07-Examples-And-Cases]]) — confession hooks.
- **Your lesson titles** — if you wrote specific titles ([[02-Build/03-Outlining]]), many
  work as hooks with light editing.
- **Three-star reviews** of competing products — "here's what nobody explains about X".
- **Your support inbox**, once you have one. Every repeated question is a post.

You are not inventing content. You are re-cutting material you already wrote.

---

## Testing

Post the same idea with different hooks, a week apart. Watch retention, not views.

Most platforms show you a retention graph. The number that matters: **what percentage
watched past three seconds.** Under 50% and the hook failed, regardless of total views.
Above 70% and the hook is working even if the post did not travel — which tells you the
problem is elsewhere.

This is the most actionable number available to you. See [[08-Reading-Analytics]].

---

## Action Items

1. **Write 30 hooks** in `Hooks.md`, drawn from `Their Words.md` and your lesson titles.
   Do it in one sitting; volume beats deliberation here.
2. **Mark the five strongest.** Those are your next five posts.
3. **Check each against the "don't" table.** Rewrite anything that describes the post
   rather than starting it.
4. **Write each in all three modes** — spoken, on-screen text, opening visual.
5. **After ten posts, check retention at three seconds.** Below 50% consistently means
   hooks, not content, are your problem.
