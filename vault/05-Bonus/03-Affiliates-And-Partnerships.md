# 5.3 — Affiliates and partnerships

**11 minutes · Module 5 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

---

## The idea

Other people sell your course and keep a share. You pay only on results, so the downside
is zero and the upside is reach you could not have built yourself.

It works. It also has a failure mode — becoming the kind of operation where everyone is
selling everyone else's course to each other — and this lesson is mostly about staying on
the right side of that.

---

## When you are ready

**Not before you have sales and a refund rate under about 8%.**

Two reasons. A product that does not convert will not convert for an affiliate either, and
you will burn a relationship proving it. And a product that refunds heavily makes the
affiliate look bad to their own audience, which is the thing they are actually protecting.

Affiliates amplify what exists. They do not fix what does not work.

---

## What to pay

**30–50% for digital products** is standard, and 40% is a reasonable default.

That sounds enormous until you notice your marginal cost is zero. £47 × 40% = £18.80 to
them, £28.20 to you, on a sale you would not otherwise have had.

Pay after your refund window closes — 14 or 30 days. Paying immediately means clawing
money back when a sale refunds, which is awkward and sours the relationship.

---

## Who to approach

In order of how well it works:

### 1. Your own customers

The best affiliates you will ever have. They have used it, they can speak to it honestly,
and their recommendation is credible precisely because they paid for it.

Offer it in your day-45 email. Keep it simple and make it optional.

### 2. People adjacent to your topic

Someone teaching listing optimisation for Etsy is a natural partner for a course on Etsy
product photography. Same audience, complementary rather than competing.

**Approach with a specific reason**, not a template:

> "I teach product photography for Etsy sellers. Your listings course covers the copy side
> and points people at photos as a gap — that's exactly what mine covers. Happy to send
> you a free copy to look at. If you think it's any good, 40% on anything you refer."

The free copy is important. Nobody credible recommends something they have not seen.

### 3. Communities and newsletters

Niche newsletters and community owners often have far more purchasing influence than their
subscriber count suggests, because the trust is high.

Same approach: free copy first, honest offer, no pressure.

---

## What not to do

**Do not recruit affiliates who have not used it.** They cannot speak to it honestly, so
their promotion is generic, and generic promotion from a trusted person damages the trust
rather than transferring it.

**Do not build a multi-level structure.** Affiliates earning from affiliates they recruit
is the defining feature of an MLM, and it attracts exactly the regulatory and reputational
attention you would expect.

**Do not let affiliates make claims you cannot support.** This is the one that catches
people out: **you are responsible for what your affiliates say about your product.** An
affiliate promising "£5k a month" has made an earnings claim on your behalf, and the FTC
treats it as yours ([[01-Mindset/06-Ethics-And-Claims]]).

Give them approved copy. Say plainly what may not be claimed. Check occasionally.

**And disclosure is mandatory.** Affiliates must clearly disclose the relationship —
"I earn a commission if you buy through this link". Not buried, not in a footer. Tell
them this is a condition, because their failure becomes your problem.

---

## Running it practically

For a handful of affiliates, you do not need software:

- Give each a unique link: `yoursite.com/?utm_source=affiliate-jane`
- The admin dashboard's **by-source** table shows sales per source
- Pay monthly by bank transfer or PayPal
- Keep a simple spreadsheet

That is genuinely sufficient up to perhaps ten affiliates. Beyond that, look at a platform
that handles tracking and payouts.

Do not build affiliate software. It is a distraction dressed as infrastructure.

---

## The affiliate pack

Make it easy to say yes. One document containing:

- What the course is and who it is for, in two paragraphs
- Their commission and when it is paid
- Their unique link
- **Approved claims** — what they may say
- **Prohibited claims** — what they may not, especially anything about income
- The disclosure requirement, stated as a condition
- Two or three example posts they can adapt
- Your contact

Fifteen minutes to write, and it removes the friction that makes people not bother.

---

## Partnerships that are not affiliate deals

Often better, and frequently overlooked:

**Guest content.** Write for their newsletter, appear on their podcast. No commission,
just reach — and it positions you as a peer rather than a vendor.

**Bundles.** Two or three complementary courses sold together for a limited period. Each
of you promotes to your own list, everyone reaches a new audience.

**Cross-recommendation.** Simply recommending each other's work where genuinely relevant.
No money changes hands, and the trust transfer is higher precisely because there is no
commission.

That last one is underrated. A recommendation with no financial interest is worth more
than one with, and everyone knows it.

---

## Action Items

1. **Check you are ready:** sales happening, refund rate under 8%.
2. **Set your commission** — 40% unless you have a reason — and your payout timing, after
   the refund window.
3. **Write the affiliate pack.** Fifteen minutes. Include the prohibited claims section.
4. **Offer it to your customers** in the day-45 email.
5. **Approach three adjacent creators** with a specific reason and a free copy.
6. **Set up UTM links** so the dashboard tracks each affiliate's sales.
