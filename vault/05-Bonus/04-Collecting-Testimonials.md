# 5.4 — Collecting testimonials, legally

**12 minutes · Module 5 · [[00-Module-Overview|Back to module]]**

> Read this before you put a single quote on your sales page. This is the lesson in the
> vault with the clearest legal consequences for getting it wrong.

---

## The rules, first

Since 2024, the FTC's **Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials
(16 CFR Part 465)** carries civil penalties **per violation**. The UK and EU have
equivalents under consumer protection law and the ASA codes.

Prohibited, and people do several of these casually:

- Writing a testimonial yourself and attributing it to a customer, or a person who does
  not exist
- Buying reviews
- Using a real quote out of context so it means something else
- Publishing a customer's words without permission to publish them
- Showing positive reviews while suppressing negative ones
- Presenting an exceptional result as though it were typical

And the one most often missed:

> **A testimonial quoting a result becomes YOUR claim the moment you publish it.**

"Sarah made £3,000 in her first month" on your page is not a quote you are merely
repeating. It is an earnings claim you must be able to substantiate. Attribution does not
transfer the burden.

---

## Why this matters commercially too

Set the law aside for a moment.

Your reader has seen a hundred pages with five glowing testimonials from smiling stock
photos. Their default assumption is that some of them are fake. Fabricated social proof
does not just fail to persuade — it **actively confirms the suspicion they arrived with**,
and takes your genuine claims down with it.

Payment processors also treat fake reviews as a fraud indicator. Losing Stripe ends the
business the same afternoon.

The honest version is both legal and more effective. That is a rare combination and worth
taking.

---

## The three separate permissions

Keep these distinct. Conflating them is where people go wrong without meaning to.

**1. Permission to receive feedback.** They replied to your email. That is all this means.

**2. Permission to publish it.** A separate, explicit yes — including the name you would
use. *"They said something nice in a DM"* is not permission to put their name on a sales
page.

**3. Evidence for any claim in it.** If they mention numbers, you need to have seen
something. Not because you doubt them — because you are the one who has to stand behind it.

The database in this system records all three separately, and it **refuses to publish a
testimonial with no consent on record.** That friction is deliberate.

## The collection process

### Step 1 — Ask everyone

The day-45 email in the sequence ([[03-Sell/08-Email-Sequence]]) does this automatically.

Ask openly, including for the bad answers:

> Good, bad or nothing at all — all three are useful and the last one is the most useful.

You want the negative feedback. It is how the product improves, and asking only for praise
means you learn nothing and receive less.

### Step 2 — Separate the two questions on the form

The submission form asks for the feedback and, separately, whether you may quote them
publicly. Two fields. An explicit checkbox, unticked by default.

### Step 3 — Confirm the exact wording

Before publishing, send them precisely what would appear:

> "Would you be happy with this on the page?
>
> *'[exact quote]'*
> — [Name you would use], [description]
>
> Say no or change anything — genuinely no problem either way."

This is not merely polite. It is your record that they agreed to *this* text, under *this*
name.

### Step 4 — Verify any numbers

If they claim a result:

> "Would you mind sending a screenshot? Not that I doubt you at all — it's that if I put
> a figure on my sales page I have to be able to back it up if anyone asks."

That framing is honest and lands well. Most people send it happily. If they decline, **use
the quote without the number.**

### Step 5 — Approve it

Only then mark it approved in the dashboard. It appears on the page.

## What makes a testimonial useful

Most are useless: "Great course, highly recommend!" persuades nobody because anyone could
have written it.

The useful ones are **specific** and address **a real hesitation**.

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "Amazing course!" | "I'd been putting off reshooting my range for a year. Did all 40 in an afternoon." |
| "So much value" | "The bit about ceiling lights was the thing I'd never seen anywhere else." |
| "Highly recommend" | "I almost didn't buy because I thought I needed a proper camera. You don't." |

That last one is the most valuable kind: **a customer answering the objection that nearly
stopped them buying.** It is more persuasive than anything you could write, because it
comes from someone who had the objection.

### Prompt for it

Instead of "leave a review", ask:

- What nearly stopped you buying? What happened instead?
- What is the one thing you would tell someone considering it?
- What did you do differently after?

These produce usable answers. "How did you find it?" produces "great, thanks".

---

## What to do when you have none

You will be in this position at launch. Everyone is.

**Say so.** The sales page in this system renders an honest empty state:

> I don't have testimonials to show you yet. I could write some. Plenty of pages do. I'd
> rather tell you what I actually have instead.

Then point at the proof that does exist:

- **The product is inspectable.** Full lesson list on the page, nothing hidden.
- **The risk sits with you.** Unconditional 14-day refund.
- **Your own experience**, honestly described, with the boundary stated.

This converts better than invented quotes, because the reader believes it. Candour reads
as confidence.

**Do not put placeholders on the page "temporarily".** That is precisely the illegal thing,
it is spotted, and "I was going to replace them" is not a defence.

---

## Displaying them honestly

- **Real name and a real description**, as agreed.
- **Do not imply typicality.** If a result is exceptional, either say so or do not use it.
- **"Results not typical" does not cure an unrepresentative claim.** Disclaimers do not
  rescue a misleading overall impression.
- **Do not suppress negative feedback** while showing positive. If you display reviews at
  all, display them honestly.
- **Only mark a result "verified" if you have actually seen evidence.** A verification
  badge you cannot back up is worse than no badge at all.

---

## Action Items

1. **Set up the day-45 request** so it goes out automatically.
2. **Check your form has two separate fields** — the feedback, and consent to publish.
3. **Write your confirmation template** using the step-3 wording. Keep it in
   `Templates/`.
4. **Write your screenshot request** using the step-4 framing.
5. **Audit anything already on your page.** Anything without recorded consent, or any
   number without evidence, comes down today.
6. **If you have none, use the honest empty state.** It works.
