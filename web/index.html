The actual problem

You know something worth teaching. But then what?

You've thought about selling a course before. You have the knowledge. But building one means solving four hard problems:

  • What to teach. You know too much. Which part is actually valuable?
  • How to structure it. What order? How long per lesson? Where does one end?
  • The tooling cost. Platform, payment processing, emails, dashboard. A week of setup you don't want to do.
  • Getting customers. Finishing doesn't mean anyone finds it. Distribution is a separate skill.

All four problems are solved. Someone already worked out the answers and wrote them down. That's what you're buying: not having to figure it out yourself.

Questions

The ones worth asking.

Isn't this just a course about selling courses?

It is, and that deserves a straight answer rather than a defensive one, because the instinct behind the question is sound. There's a real version of this business and a parasitic version, and they look similar from outside.

The parasitic version teaches you to sell courses about selling courses, to people who will sell courses about selling courses. Nothing of value is created at any level. That's the pattern you're right to be suspicious of.

The whole system here is built for you to teach your thing — the bookkeeping you've done for nine years, the language you speak, the software you know. Modules 1 and 2 are entirely about extracting a course from your existing experience. If your plan is to buy this and immediately sell a course about buying this, you'll find the material actively unhelpful, and I'd rather you didn't.

How much money will I make?

I don't know, and neither does anyone else selling you something in this category.

It depends on what you teach, whether people want it, how well you explain it, how much you publish, and how long you keep going after the first month of silence. Those variables swamp anything the system itself contributes. A well-executed course on a topic nobody wants earns nothing. A rough course on something people urgently need can do well.

What I can tell you concretely: the costs are small (a domain, optionally ~€20/month for an AI subscription while drafting, and 2.9% + 30¢ per sale to Stripe), the work is front-loaded, and the file you produce can be sold indefinitely without being rebuilt. That's the actual shape of the opportunity. Anyone giving you a figure is guessing, and probably not in your favour.

I don't have an audience. Is that fatal?

No, but it does change the order of operations, and Module 4 is largely about this.

With no audience, distribution is a separate project from building, and it starts the day you start writing rather than the day you finish. The realistic version: you post consistently for several weeks to near-silence, because that's what the beginning looks like for almost everyone, and then it starts compounding.

The dishonest version of this answer promises you a viral formula. There isn't one. There's a process that works if you run it long enough, and Module 4 is that process written down — including a lesson specifically on what to do when nothing is working, because that's the point at which most people stop.

What if I'm not actually an expert?

You don't need to be the best in the world. You need to be meaningfully further along than the person you're teaching, and able to remember what it was like not to know.

That second part is the real qualification, and genuine experts are often worse at it. Someone two years into a skill frequently teaches beginners better than someone twenty years in, because they still remember which bits were confusing.

The line that matters is honesty about where you stand. "Here's how I did this, and what I'd skip if I started again" is a legitimate and valuable course. "I'm a leading authority" when you aren't is how you end up with refunds and a reputation problem. Module 1 covers exactly how to position this.

How long before I have something to sell?

At 4–6 hours a week, most people have a finished first course in four to eight weeks. The spread is mostly about how long you spend deciding what to teach — the writing itself is faster than people expect once the outline exists.

Selling it is a different timeline. You can have a live page and working checkout in an afternoon with The Engine. Having people arrive at that page is the slow part, and it's measured in months of consistent posting, not days.

If you see "48 hours to a profitable course" anywhere — including in older versions of my own material — treat it as marketing rather than a forecast. You can build something in a weekend. You will not have a business by Monday.

What do I actually need to run this?

For The Method: a computer and a text editor. Obsidian is free and recommended but not required — the lessons are plain Markdown files that open anywhere.

For The Engine: a domain (~€12/year), a Stripe account (free, takes a percentage per sale), and somewhere to run a small Node.js server. Free tiers on Railway, Render or Fly work fine at launch volumes. The deployment guide walks through each, written for someone who has not deployed anything before.

You do not need to know how to code. You do need to be willing to follow a technical guide carefully and not panic at a terminal.

Do I have to show my face?

No. The course is written, and everything in the distribution module works with screen recordings, text-on-screen and voiceover.

A caveat worth having: showing your face does tend to build trust faster, and pretending otherwise would be doing you a disservice. It's a trade, not a free lunch. Plenty of people run this entirely faceless — it's just slightly slower to build the same trust.
